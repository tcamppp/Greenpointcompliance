1. The two part test we applied

Local Law 144 imposes obligations that are usually described together and satisfied separately. An employer using an automated employment decision tool on a New York City candidate must have a bias audit conducted within the prior twelve months and publish a summary of it, and must notify the candidate at least ten business days before the tool is used.

This study treats an employer as compliant only where both conditions hold. A published summary must exist and must be specific to that employer's use of the tool. A candidate notice must appear on the job listing. Failing either one is non compliance, because the statute treats them as independent obligations and each carries its own penalty.

Nothing here assesses whether any tool produces biased outcomes. The question is narrower and entirely documentary. Did the employer publish, and did the employer notify.

A finding of “not found” is not a finding of non compliance in law. It records that a diligent public search did not surface the required disclosure. A summary sitting behind a robots exclusion, on a domain the employer does not control, or at a path no sitemap enumerates would read as absent to us and to a candidate looking for it.

3. The audit is the binding failure

The headline rate of 99.7 percent understates what the data shows, because the two requirements fail at very different rates and for different reasons.

Sixty seven employers display a candidate notice. Two hundred and ninety nine have no employer specific published bias audit summary. Four more publish or link a summary produced by a tool vendor, treated separately below. The notice is the cheaper obligation, it is frequently supplied by the hiring platform rather than written by the employer, and it is often the only one attempted.

Read the third row of Table 1 again. Not one employer in the sample published an audit while omitting the notice. That zero is the most useful number in the study. An employer that gets as far as commissioning an audit reliably posts a notice as well, which means the notice is a leading indicator and the audit is the thing that separates a compliance program from a compliance gesture.

Size does not change this. Failure rates across the three headcount bands in the sample, roughly 200 to 500, 500 to 1,000 and 1,000 to 2,000 employees, sit within four percentage points of one another on the notice requirement and are effectively identical on the audit. Larger employers in this range are not doing better.

The gap between Greenhouse and Ashby is roughly eighty seven percentage points. A difference of that magnitude across 250 and 26 records is not plausibly sampling noise. During collection we observed near identical notice wording across boards on the same platform, which points to the likeliest explanation. Ashby and Lever render a standard notice at the board level. No association appears between platform and the bias audit. Failure runs at 99.6, 100.0 and 100.0 percent across the three. There is no variation to explain. An employer that changes platform should expect its notice posture to change and its audit posture to stay exactly where it is.

A platform default is not your disclosure. A notice your vendor renders may satisfy the notice requirement on its face, and it does nothing at all for the audit requirement. It can also create an internal impression that the obligation has been handled. Sixty six employers in this sample display a notice and have published no audit, which is what that impression looks like in the data.

5. Why a vendor summary usually does not count

Four employers publish or link a bias audit summary produced by a tool vendor rather than by or for the employer. None is counted as compliant on the audit requirement.

Under the Department of Consumer and Worker Protection rules, the employer is responsible for the bias audit. Section 5-302(a) permits an employer to rely on an audit using the historical data of other employers only in narrow circumstances: where the employer supplied its own historical data from its use of the tool to the independent auditor, or where the employer has never used the tool. An employer that has run a tool for a year, contributed no data to the audit, and points candidates to the vendor's aggregate summary sits inside neither exception.

Content matters as much as provenance. Section 5-303(a)(1) requires the published summary to state the source and explanation of the data used, the number of individuals assessed who fall in an unknown category, the number of applicants or candidates, the selection or scoring rates as applicable, and the impact ratios for all categories. A vendor summary computed across a pooled population of many customers reports the vendor's aggregate figures. It cannot report your selection rates, because your applicants are a fraction of the pool.

Among those four, one links a summary whose stated population runs to roughly ten million applicant assessments pooled across every customer of the tool. Another links two summaries hosted on domains the employer does not control, both unreachable to an automated reader at the time of collection. A third publishes a summary carrying an explicit statement from the auditor that the report reflects controlled test conditions and should not be read as a compliance determination.

Each of those employers is closer to compliance than the 295 with nothing at all. Each is also exposed in a particular way. The published artifact creates an appearance of diligence the artifact does not support, and the employer may believe the requirement is discharged when it is not.

6. Modeled statutory exposure

Section 20-872 of the Administrative Code sets a civil penalty of not more than $500 for a first violation and each additional violation occurring on the same day, then $500 to $1,500 for each subsequent violation. Each day a tool is used in violation of Section 20-871(a) is a separate violation. Each failure to provide notice under Section 20-871(b) is a separate violation.

What follows is a bounding exercise across the observed sample. It is not a prediction of enforcement. Every input is stated rather than implied.

The 299 non compliant employers carry 2,945 live New York roles between them. The median employer posts six. Thirty three post twenty or more, and the largest posts 77. Per role models concentrate exposure heavily in that small group, and any figure derived from the role count should be read with that concentration in mind.

Counting only roles physically located in New York City understates the exposed population. The rule reaches employers using a covered tool for jobs located in the city at least part time, and for remote positions where the location associated with the position is an office in New York City. Guidance has not fully settled what association means in practice. Because the dataset does not record remote roles separately, this study does not model them, which makes the role counts a floor.

Why this is a ceiling and not a forecast. The Comptroller's December 2025 audit found that DCWP received two AEDT complaints across two years, that 75 percent of test calls to 311 were routed elsewhere, and that seventeen instances of potential non compliance sat inside a set of 32 companies the department had cleared with one finding. Statutory exposure is very large and largely theoretical today. Enforcement capacity has been documented as weak by the state's own auditor, and that same audit creates pressure to strengthen it. Treat the maximum figure as a bound on the downside, not an expected value, while recognizing that the gap between the bound and observed enforcement is a policy choice that can change.

7. What compliance actually looks like

One employer in this sample meets both requirements. IEX Group, the operator of the Investors Exchange, is named here because naming a positive model is useful and carries none of the risk that attaches to naming a shortfall. No other employer is identified anywhere in the study or in the published PDF.

Tool
Named in the disclosure, along with the vendor supplying it
Auditor
Named, and independent of the tool
Dates
Audit date of 3 February 2026 and distribution date of 27 April 2026, placing distribution inside the twelve month window
Notice
A dedicated notice reachable from the job posting, describing what the tool does, offering an alternative selection process, and giving a named contact for the request
Timing
Published on the careers page and carried on the posting ahead of any screening, which is the sequencing the ten business day rule requires

Naming the tool, the auditor and both dates is the part most employers omit entirely. Timing is the element worth copying, because a notice added after screening has begun does not cure the earlier use.

Candor about the limits of the example is warranted. The underlying vendor report supporting the IEX disclosure states that it is provided for demonstration purposes, reflects behavior under controlled test conditions, and should not be read as a formal certification. Its sample sizes are uniform across demographic cells in a pattern characteristic of synthetic test data rather than live applicant flow. Section 5-302(b) permits reliance on test data where insufficient historical data exists to support a statistically significant audit, so a test data audit is not automatically deficient. An employer building on this model should reach for its own applicant data where volume supports it.

8. Method, limits and corrections

Data was collected between 2 and 7 September 2026 from publicly available sources. No login, paywall or private access was used. Collection ran in three passes. The first searched each employer's own corporate domain for a published summary, using keyword searches, enumeration of the sitemap and robots file, and direct retrieval of legal and careers pages. The second examined the applicant tracking board for platform level notices. The third opened an individual job posting located in New York City or listed as remote and read its full text.

Every affirmative finding was retrieved and read a second time before entry. That verification step was added after an early error in which a notice reported on one employer's posting proved to originate from a different employer's board. The affected record was corrected before analysis.

Four limits bear on how these figures should be used.

  1. A posting level observation reflects one posting on one day. An employer may carry a notice on other postings this study did not open, so notice failure rates are an upper bound on failure at the employer level.
  2. Absence of a published summary is evidence that none was found, not proof that none exists.
  3. The sample covers employers of roughly 200 to 2,000 people with live New York postings. It does not extend above or below that range.
  4. Industry categories with fewer than ten employers appear in the PDF marked as unreliable and should be read as descriptive of those specific employers rather than as industry estimates.
